How to focus while reading when your mind keeps wandering

What attention research actually says, and the small habits that help you stay with a page.

By Monty Montgomery · September 26, 2026 · 4 min read

You sit down with a book you genuinely want to read, and by the third paragraph your hand is already reaching for your phone. Or you get through a whole page and realize your eyes moved across every line while your mind was planning dinner.

If that sounds familiar, you're in large company. Reading for pleasure has been declining for two decades, as we described in Too long? Read it anyway., and many people who still love books find it harder to settle into one. The research on attention is more hopeful than the headlines suggest, though, and a few small habits make a real difference.

What the research shows about attention

Gloria Mark, a professor emerita of informatics at UC Irvine, has measured how long people stay on one screen before switching to something else. In her early studies the average was about two and a half minutes, and by the years 2016 to 2020 it had fallen to around 47 seconds. She also notes that getting back on track after an interruption can take close to half an hour.

Her studies follow how people move between screens during the workday, which makes them a record of a habit many of us have practiced for years: the quick glance at something else. That framing is encouraging, because habits that were learned can be reshaped. Attention behaves much like the other mental skills we rely on, which tend to weaken when we stop using them, a pattern we look at in The skills you don't notice losing.

Learn when your focus is strongest

In the same interview, Mark suggests learning your own rhythm of attention, for example by keeping a short diary of when you feel most and least focused, and saving your most important work for the times your attention peaks. Reading fits into that plan well. If your mind is sharpest in the morning, a few pages before you open email will go further than an hour at the end of a long day.

Make the distraction harder to reach

Putting your phone in another room adds a few seconds of friction, and those seconds often give the urge to check it time to pass. If you read on your phone, turn on a focus mode that silences notifications for the length of your session, and close the apps you tend to drift toward.

Start smaller than you think you need to

Long sessions are where focus tends to fall apart, so begin with something you can finish. A short article, a single chapter or a passage of a few hundred words gives your attention a clear finish line. Once a short session feels comfortable, you can stretch it gradually, the same way you'd add distance to a run.

Give your reading a purpose

Your mind wanders less when it has a job to do. Before you start, pick one question you want the text to answer, such as what the author is arguing or why a character made a particular choice. When you notice yourself drifting, that question gives you somewhere to return. Afterward, try saying the answer to yourself in a sentence, which helps the reading stay with you. That last step is one of the habits in our guide on how to improve reading comprehension as an adult.

A daily rep for your attention

DidRead was designed with these ideas in mind. Each day there's one short passage and a single question to answer once you reach the end, about five minutes in all. The passage is short enough to finish in one sitting, and the question gives your attention a reason to stay on the page. Over a few weeks, staying with a text for five minutes starts to feel ordinary again, which is the foundation for reading longer things with ease.
